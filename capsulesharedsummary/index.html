Permission Inheritance Rules
📌
The GP is the default coordinating practitioner and owns the shared summary. Any new content added by allied health is flagged for GP review before appearing in the shared view.
⚡
Flags can bypass GP review only for time-sensitive safety concerns (e.g. a physio flagging a fall risk that affects prescribing). These are marked as Unreviewed and the GP is notified immediately.
🔒
Psychology is opt-out by default. Psychologists never see the shared summary unless the patient grants explicit written consent for a specific disclosure. Even then, only a single summary flag can be shared — not session notes.

Design Rationale

The Capsule Shared Patient Summary solves a real coordination failure — practitioners working in silos, patients acting as the information bridge, duplicated and conflicting care — without introducing the risks that typically kill these projects: information overload, liability-driven note inflation, or privacy breaches.

Here's how every key design decision was made.

1. Summaries, not notes

The central design principle is that the shared view is a summary layer, not a document repository. Raw consultation notes never appear. Instead, only structured, clinician-curated facts are surfaced: diagnoses, medications, goals, cross-discipline flags.

This immediately eliminates the biggest liability risk: practitioners being bound by another clinician's working hypotheses, narrative impressions, or unconfirmed differentials. What you see is what has been confirmed and promoted by a named clinician.

2. The GP as coordinator, not gatekeeper

The coordinating GP controls what appears in the shared view, but this is not a bottleneck model. Allied health practitioners contribute structured flags — a physio flags a mobility restriction, a dietitian flags a nutritional concern — and these appear immediately in their contribution queue while awaiting GP ratification for the shared layer.

For safety-critical flags, the system allows immediate surfacing with an "Unreviewed" indicator and a GP notification. This preserves speed without surrendering oversight.

3. Role-scoped views eliminate information overload

Each practitioner only sees what is clinically relevant to them. A physiotherapist doesn't need to know about a patient's medication history for hypertension — they need to know about movement restrictions, pain flags, and any medications that affect exercise tolerance. The system calculates this automatically by role, and practitioners cannot access data outside their scope.

This is not just about privacy — it's about reducing cognitive load. A summary that shows a physio everything the GP knows is useless noise. A summary that shows them exactly what they need to treat the patient safely is a clinical superpower.

4. Psychology is fully siloed by default

Mental health records are fundamentally different in nature and in law. Psychologists cannot see the shared summary, and their notes are never surfaced to other practitioners — even the coordinating GP — without explicit patient consent for a specific, scoped disclosure.

The only exception is a voluntary "awareness flag" — a binary signal the psychologist can choose to raise (with patient consent) that simply says "this patient is engaged in mental health support." No content, no detail. Just enough for the GP to avoid prescribing contraindicated medications or dismissing unexplained symptoms.

5. How this avoids CYA note inflation

Defensive documentation happens when practitioners feel they will be judged by what they wrote — or blamed for what they didn't. Capsule's architecture directly attacks the conditions that cause CYA inflation:

❌ CYA TRIGGER
"Patient reports pain at 7/10. Advised to reduce activity. Physio informed. Note: unclear if cardiac origin ruled out. Further investigation may be warranted. Not my area."
✓ CAPSULE MODEL
Physio flags: Mobility restriction — load-bearing activity. GP to advise on cardiac clearance before next session. Structured. Scoped. Actionable.
Structured fields, not free text
Contributions to the shared view are structured — flags, goals, diagnoses — not prose. You can't write an essay in a flag field. The format enforces brevity and removes the temptation to narrate defensively.
Attribution without accusation
Every item in the shared view is attributed to its author. This creates accountability without blame — a physio's flag belongs to the physio, and is clearly not the GP's clinical judgment. Practitioners aren't taking on responsibility for each other's domains.
No raw notes in the shared view
Because raw notes never appear, practitioners don't write the shared summary — they curate it. This decouples the clinical documentation process (where CYA inflation lives) from the coordination layer entirely.
Audit log, not judgment log
The access log records who viewed what, and when. This is for transparency and patient safety — not for attributing blame. Practitioners know they're not being surveilled on clinical judgment, only on access.

6. Why this is legally survivable

The model is designed to survive medicolegal scrutiny because of three properties:

Explicit attribution. Every shared item names a clinician and a timestamp. There is no ambiguity about who asserted what.

Consent-gated sensitive fields. Psychology content requires written patient consent for any disclosure. This is logged and enforced at the system level.

Scoped responsibility. Practitioners are only shown, and only contribute to, sections within their clinical scope. A dietitian seeing a medication list cannot be construed as taking responsibility for it.
